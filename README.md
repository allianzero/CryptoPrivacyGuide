# CryptoPrivacyGuide

A beginner-friendly guide to privacy in crypto: what is public on a blockchain, what is not, and how to protect your privacy with simple, safe habits.

This guide is for education only. It does not help you hide illegal activity, evade the law, or break the rules of an exchange or service. Always follow the law in your country.

## 1. The first rule: blockchains are public by default

Most blockchains, such as Bitcoin and Ethereum, are public ledgers. Anyone can look up:

- A wallet address
- Its balance and transaction history
- Who it sent funds to, and who sent funds to it

Your name is usually not written on the blockchain. But an address can still be linked to you if, for example, you post it publicly, use it on an exchange account in your real name, or reuse it everywhere.

Privacy in crypto is therefore not about being invisible. It is about reducing unnecessary links between your identity and your on-chain activity.

## 2. Addresses: use fresh ones when you can

- Do not reuse the same receiving address for every payment if your wallet supports fresh addresses.
- Do not post your main wallet address publicly unless you accept that its history becomes public.
- Keep a separate wallet for public activity (for example, donations or public posts) and a separate wallet for private holdings, if that fits your needs.
- Never share a seed phrase or private key with anyone, for any reason. No real support team will ever ask for it.

## 3. What leaks privacy, even without a name

- **Address reuse:** one address used everywhere links all of those payments together.
- **Exchange withdrawals:** withdrawing from an exchange to your wallet can link that wallet to your exchange account in the exchange's own records.
- **ENS names and public profiles:** a human-readable name tied to an address makes that address easy to identify.
- **NFTs and public posts:** posting a screenshot of your wallet, or bragging about an NFT you own, can link your social account to your address.
- **IP addresses and trackers:** a wallet app or website can log your IP address. Use official apps and be careful with unknown dApps and links.
- **Small, unique amounts:** a very specific amount sent at a very specific time can be easy to trace, even without a name.

## 4. Safe habits checklist

1. Use a reputable wallet, keep its software updated, and back up your seed phrase offline, on paper, in a safe place.
2. Use strong, unique passwords and two-factor authentication (2FA) for exchange and email accounts.
3. Before you connect a wallet to a website, check the URL carefully and revoke permissions you no longer use.
4. Send a small test transaction first when you use a new address or network.
5. Think before you post: a wallet address, transaction hash, or balance screenshot can identify you.
6. Keep records for tax and accounting where required, even if you value privacy. Privacy is not a reason to skip legal duties.
7. Be sceptical of "privacy tools" that promise total anonymity. Some are scams, some are broken, and some are illegal to use in your jurisdiction. Research before you trust any tool with your funds.

## 5. Privacy tools, explained simply

- **Fresh addresses and separate wallets:** the simplest, safest first step. No extra software, no extra risk.
- **Privacy-focused networks and coins:** some networks are designed with stronger privacy by default. They have their own risks, fees, and legal rules, so learn them before using them.
- **Mixers and tumblers:** services that mix funds to break links. They are high-risk, often associated with scams or crime, and restricted or illegal in many places. This guide does not recommend them.
- **VPNs and Tor:** these can hide your IP address from a website, but they do not hide your on-chain transactions. They are a network-privacy tool, not a blockchain-privacy tool.

## 6. A simple example

Bad habit: you post your main wallet address on social media, then use the same address to receive salary, pay friends, and buy NFTs. Anyone who sees the post can now see all of that history.

Better habit: you keep a public-facing wallet for public posts, and a separate wallet for personal holdings. You do not link them publicly, and you do not post balances or transaction screenshots.

## 7. Frequently asked questions

**Is crypto anonymous?**
No. Most crypto is pseudonymous: addresses are not names, but activity is public and can often be linked to a person.

**Does deleting a wallet app delete my history?**
No. The blockchain keeps the history. Deleting an app only removes your local access to it.

**Can I make an old transaction private?**
No. You cannot edit or delete a confirmed blockchain transaction. Privacy habits only help future activity.

**Is using a new wallet enough?**
It helps, but if you fund the new wallet directly from an old wallet in a visible way, the link remains visible on-chain.

## 8. Glossary

- **Address:** a public identifier you share to receive funds, like an account number.
- **Private key / seed phrase:** the secret that controls a wallet. Keep it secret, keep it offline.
- **On-chain:** recorded on the blockchain, visible to anyone.
- **Pseudonymous:** identified by an address or nickname, not by a real name, but still traceable.
